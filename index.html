Statement

This page is my life as a dataset: 53 streams across six strands, from Cape Town in 1981 to Givatayim today. Work, education, places lived, places worked, personal milestones and everything else.

The first state is order. It follows the Swiss tradition of Müller-Brockmann: a twelve-column grid, one typeface, a strict time axis, and every chapter drawn at exactly the same weight. Each stream sits in its strand, stacked by start date, and nothing touches anything else. In this state, years at a newspaper weigh as much as a single guest lecture. The chart is true and clean, and it says almost nothing about what those years were like.

The second state, This time with feelings, adds two columns that only I could fill in. One is intensity: how much each chapter took from me, from 1 to 5. The other is the overall feeling: good, neutral or bad. The streams swell with intensity until the strands no longer fit their rows and spill into one another. Bad chapters are dotted, neutral ones hatched, good ones left open. The grid stays underneath, so the break is always read against the order it breaks.

No model could have produced this layer. It does not know which chapters were good, which were bad, or how much each one took. The disorder carries information.

I built the page in conversation with an AI, and it kept pulling toward the generic AI look: colour gradients for intensity, soft rounded shapes, a hand-drawn filter, decorative fonts. I tried most of these and removed them: the pink-to-bordeaux scale, the cyan-to-green and blue-to-red ramps, the handwriting font, the wobble. What stayed is closer to newspaper information graphics than to an app: white lines on black, two patterns, Helvetica, and the names written directly on the data. There are no cards, no hero image, no gradients and no marketing copy.

Switching between the two states is the point. Without feelings, the record is accurate and empty. With feelings, it is crowded and harder to read, and it looks like a life.
